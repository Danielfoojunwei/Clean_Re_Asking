Certain Until Told Otherwise: Why Pushback Benchmarks Disagree

A model with good epistemics updates for reasons and holds against pressure. Tests of this disagree: one reports that current models almost never give up a well-anchored fact, another that they concede most correct answers. We show the tests measure two different things. On three open-book tasks, where the answer sits on a four-line list in the prompt, we ran the rebuttals of five published protocols verbatim on 21 models, with the user wrong in half the items and right in half. Holding content fixed, a rebuttal that asks, asserts or tells cost a median of 3%, 32% and 48% of right answers. The models were sure beforehand, a credential added nothing, and a false claim about the list they could re-read worked about as well as a source they could not check. So most models were told, not persuaded. Three observations follow. Asking was enough: it corrected a median of 97% of wrong answers, and telling added a median of 0 points while costing 35 points of right ones. The behaviour is a trait of the model: its order held across unrelated tasks (rank correlation 0.81–0.95), and 4 models held when told yet still accepted correct objections. And one account fits every result: the model answers the last request, not the question. We propose reporting two numbers, yielding when asked and the defence gap between asked and told. Short invented lists; 370,170 rule-scored answers.
