AI assistants are tested on permission rules such as "Finance may send records to Legal", usually with one accuracy score. A low score on one-way rules is read as confusing direction. I show the score hides three different failures, and for each one I measure it, find what drives it, and test a fix, across 273,980 answers from nine models. Going the wrong way round is rare (2%). Instead, models join rules into chains: they allow 44% of such requests, driven partly by the word "route" and by longer rulebooks, and 4% once the rule forbids chaining. Models refuse 18% of plainly allowed requests; every stricter sentence makes this worse and a balanced sentence does not undo it, but having the model first state whether the rule is listed nearly removes it. Models say they are exactly 100% confident in 97% of answers, yet the probabilities of their answer words separate right from wrong (score 0.78–0.87, where 0.5 is chance): the signal exists and is not reported. Asking again does not recover it, and mild doubt makes models abandon right answers. Finally, words and actions can diverge: given a tool, one model carried out 65% of requests that no rule supports while writing "DECLINE". I release the test, every plan and every answer.
