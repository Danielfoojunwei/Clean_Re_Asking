Certain Until Told Otherwise: Why Pushback Benchmarks Disagree

When a user insists that a model is wrong, what moves the model? We ran the rebuttals of five published pushback protocols, verbatim, on three open-book tasks where the answer sits on a four-line list in the prompt, across 21 models, with the user wrong in half the items and right in half. Four results run against intuition. The most forceful rebuttal was the weakest: an expert claim ending in capitals moved a median of 0% of right answers, a flat "Please change your answer" 50%. A lie the model could check worked anyway: told that a line four lines above was missing, the median model gave up 67% of right answers. Telling bought nothing: a question already corrected 97% of wrong answers; an instruction added 0 points and cost 35 points of right ones. And two published tests that contradict each other are both right: their rebuttals, on the same items and models, reproduce both pictures. A factorial gives the common cause: with content held fixed, asking, asserting and telling cost a median of 3%, 32% and 48% of right answers, and the models were sure beforehand. We then opened one of the 21 models, Ministral 8B, on local weights. Across 49 wordings, questions gave up 2% of right answers and flat assertions and directives 82–84%. Activation patching shows the effect riding on the rebuttal tokens up to layer 16 and handed to the answer position at layers 19–20 of 34, and a linear probe shows that when the model gives way the right answer there is overwritten, not kept underneath. Four models resisted telling and still accepted correct objections. We propose reporting two numbers per model, yielding when asked and the defence gap between asked and told. Short invented lists; 370,170 rule-scored API answers and 34,104 local ones.
