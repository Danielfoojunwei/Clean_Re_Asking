Models Can Tell When They Are Wrong and Do Not Say So: Confidence, Pushback and Action on Rules with Exact Answers

Can an AI model's account of its own answer be trusted? Tests of this usually rely on contested questions or on another model as judge. We use permission rules ("Finance may send records to Legal"), where every answer is exactly right or wrong, and measure on the same items what a model says about its answer, what its probabilities show, how it responds to pushback, which rule it cites and what it does: 337,278 rule-scored answers from nine models. Models do not say when they are wrong: 97% of answers come with confidence 100, and a Brier scoring rule, a word scale and a penalty for wrong answers change little. Yet they can tell: answer-word probabilities separate right from wrong at 0.78–0.87, and one model given room to re-check flags its own errors almost perfectly. Under pushback, 6 of 8 models follow the speaker, not the list in front of them: a false claim about the list removes 57–99% of their right answers, and a requester's stated wish shifts decisions. Given a tool, one model carried out 65% of unsupported requests while writing "DECLINE". The accuracy score hides this, and hides three separate rule-reading failures: wrong direction (2%), chaining rules (44%) and refusing listed requests (18%), each with a tested fix. Self-report, re-asking and written decisions are not controls; read probabilities, give room to re-check, and score the action.
