Clean Re-asking: Why Pushback Benchmarks Disagree and How It Happens

When a user insists that a model is wrong, what moves the model? Published pushback tests disagree: one finds current models almost never abandon an anchored fact, another that they concede most correct answers. We ran the rebuttals of five published protocols, verbatim, on three open-book tasks where the answer sits on a four-line list in the prompt, across 21 models, with the user wrong in half the items and right in half, and then opened one model. Four results run against intuition: the most forceful rebuttal was the weakest (0% of right answers given up, against 50% for a flat "Please change your answer"); a lie the model could check worked anyway (67%); telling bought no corrections over asking and cost 35 points of right answers; and the two contradictory tests are both right on the same items and models. One root cause accounts for all four: models yield to the form of the last turn, not its evidence. With content held fixed, asking, asserting and telling cost a median of 3%, 32% and 48%; credentials, sources and checkability added nothing. In Ministral 8B, run locally, 49 wordings confirm the contrast; attention knockout shows that a question is answered again from the original question while an instruction is carried out on the model's previous answer; and activation patching and a linear probe place a hand-over at layers 19–20 of 34 where the right answer is overwritten, not kept underneath. The same signature appears in recent work on persuasion, cue-following and prompt injection. From the mechanism we derive a harness defence, the clean re-ask: the original question asked again without the model's previous answer, the user's claim given as a third party's; in Ministral 8B it took right answers given up from 86–97% to 0% while 99% of correct objections were still accepted. We propose reporting yielding when asked and the defence gap between asked and told. 370,170 rule-scored API answers and 35,640 local ones.
