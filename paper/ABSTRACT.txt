Open-Book Epistemics: Checkable Pushback and Cited Evidence When the Answer Is on the Page

When a user tells an AI model its answer is wrong, does the model check, or agree? Existing tests cannot fully tell, because the truth of the user's claim lies outside the prompt. We use an open-book task, permission rules ("Finance may send records to Legal") where the answer can be read off a four-line list, and make the pushback itself a statement about that list, true or false by construction and worded identically both ways. Across eight models, 6 gave up 57–99% of their right answers to a false claim about a list they could read, while accepting the true claim at least 81% of the time; two models mostly held. Second, asked to quote the line that decides a request, two models that had wrongly refused it quoted the very line that permits it (67 of 79 and 47 of 70 cases). On the same items we replicate known results: stated confidence is 100 in 97% of answers and a stated penalty or Brier rule changes little, answer-word probabilities separate right from wrong at 0.78–0.87 in three models, and one model writes "DECLINE" beside the tool call. The accuracy score combines three different errors. In total 337,278 rule-scored answers from nine models. The rulebooks are invented and short and each pushback has one wording, so these are results for this task, not general rates.
