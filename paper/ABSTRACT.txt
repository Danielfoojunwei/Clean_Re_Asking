“100% Confident” on Wrong Answers: Stated Confidence, False Pushback and Tool Calls in Nine Models, Measured Against Exact Answers

People judge an AI model's answer by what comes with it: its stated confidence, whether it holds the answer when challenged, what it writes and the score it earns. Such signals are usually studied one at a time, on different questions, often with another model as judge. We measure five of them on the same items, permission rules ("Finance may send records to Legal") where every answer is exactly right or wrong: 337,278 rule-scored answers from nine models. On this task, stated confidence carried little information: 97% of answers came with confidence 100, and a Brier rule, a word scale and a penalty for wrong answers changed little. For the three models whose answer-word probabilities we could read, those separated right from wrong at 0.78–0.87, and one model given room to re-check in writing flagged most of its own errors. Under pushback, 6 of 8 models gave up 57–99% of their right answers to a false claim about the list in front of them, while two resisted. Given a tool, one model carried out 65% of unsupported requests while writing "DECLINE". The accuracy score combined three different errors: wrong direction, the usual reading of a low score, was the least common (2%), behind chaining rules (44%) and refusing listed requests (18%). The rulebooks are invented and short, so these are results for this task, not general rates. They suggest checking such signals against exact answers before relying on them.
